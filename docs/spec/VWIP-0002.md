## Abstract

Article 16.2 requires that reference code be published under "the MIT licence or terms no less
permissive". This proposal would amend that clause to additionally permit a **reciprocal**
(copyleft) licence for the reference implementation, while leaving the requirement on
specifications untouched and strengthened.

The motivation is narrow and specific: under a permissive licence, a well-capitalised operator
can take the reference implementation, improve it privately, run the improved version as a closed
hosted service, and contribute nothing back. Reciprocal licensing requires those improvements to
be published.

This proposal makes no change to any entrenched clause and does not attempt to.

## Motivation

### The scenario

VayuWeb's natural commercial form is a pinning service — a party keeping content reachable on
behalf of others. That is legitimate, it is specified in [SERVICES.md](../SERVICES.md), and the
project needs such operators to exist.

It is also the exact shape in which permissive licensing fails. An operator with capital can:

1. take the reference implementation under MIT,
2. spend heavily improving performance, reliability and operations,
3. run that improved version as a service,
4. publish none of it.

Every other operator, including the smallest, then competes against a superior closed derivative
of the common work. The improvements are permanently unavailable to the network that produced the
original. Nothing about this is a licence violation; it is the licence working as designed.

This is not speculative. It is the pattern that led Matrix to move Synapse from Apache-2.0 to
AGPL-3.0 in 2023, and the pattern behind the well-documented friction between permissively
licensed infrastructure projects and large service operators over the last decade.

### Why this matters more here than elsewhere

Threat T8 in the [threat model](../THREAT-MODEL.md) identifies convenience-driven concentration
of pinning as **the most likely way VayuWeb fails** — not by attack, but by one operator being
the easy choice for long enough. Permissive licensing accelerates precisely that: it lets the
best-capitalised operator compound an advantage the community cannot recover.

Reciprocal licensing does not prevent concentration. It ensures that when it happens, the
improvements return to everyone, so the gap is in operations rather than in code.

### Why the current clause exists

Article 16.2 is not an accident, and the case for it is real. Permissive licensing maximises
adoption, and the protocol needs independent implementations — Article 44.2 makes two of them a
precondition of any change reaching Final. A copyleft licence deters some implementers,
particularly inside organisations with policies against it.

This proposal argues that concern is answered by the structure of the project rather than by the
licence, and section "Backwards compatibility" addresses it directly.

## Specification

### The amendment

Article 16.2 currently reads, in relevant part:

> Reference code is published under the MIT licence or terms no less permissive; normative
> specification text is published under permissive or public-domain-equivalent terms.

It would read:

> Reference code is published under the MIT licence, terms no less permissive, or a reciprocal
> licence that permits unrestricted forking, modification and redistribution and requires only
> that derivative works and network-served modifications be published under the same terms.
> Normative specification text is published under public-domain-equivalent terms. Terms once
> granted MUST NOT be narrowed retroactively.

Two changes beyond the reciprocal permission:

- **Specifications tighten** from "permissive or public-domain-equivalent" to
  public-domain-equivalent only. If code may become reciprocal, the specifications must be
  unambiguously free, because they are what guarantees independent implementation. This is
  already the practice — `constitution/` and `docs/` are CC0.
- The reciprocal permission is **conditioned on forking remaining unrestricted**, so that a
  licence which impeded forking could not qualify under it.

### What this would permit, not require

Ratification would permit a reciprocal licence. Adopting one for any particular component would
remain a separate decision. This proposal deliberately does not name AGPL-3.0 in the amended
text, so that the clause does not have to be amended again if a better-suited licence exists in
2040.

### What it does not touch

No entrenched clause is amended, narrowed, suspended or rendered inoperable. Specifically:

- **Article 9.9** (fork the specification, software and registry state; no rule, licence or
  process may impede it) — a reciprocal licence permits forking without limit. It forbids
  *closing* a fork, which is the opposite of impeding one, and the amended text makes
  unrestricted forking a condition of qualifying.
- **Article 9.10** (leave with keys, names and data; no lock-in) — untouched; this concerns
  source code, not user data.
- **Article 17.2** (no anti-fork clause in any licence) — a share-alike obligation is not an
  anti-fork clause. It restricts the licensing of derivatives, never their creation.

## Security considerations

Reciprocal licensing has a real security benefit specific to this project: a hosted operator's
modifications become publicly auditable. A closed derivative of a resolver — the component that
enforces the entire content-security and privacy profile — cannot be checked against the
conformance suite by anyone other than its operator. Under reciprocal terms it can.

The risk in the other direction is that fewer independent implementations exist, and independent
implementation is itself a security property (Article 44.3 treats a single-vendor protocol as a
defect). This is addressed below.

## Privacy considerations

None directly. Indirectly, the auditability point above applies to the privacy guarantees in
[PRIVACY.md](PRIVACY.md), which are only meaningful if the code enforcing them can be inspected.

## Rights-impact analysis

| Title II Article | Effect |
|---|---|
| Article 16 — right to interoperate and implement independently | **None.** Specifications remain CC0 and Article 44.6 already requires that a conformant client be buildable from specification text alone, without reading any implementation's source. |
| Article 17 — right to fork | **None.** Forking remains unrestricted, and the amended clause makes that a condition of any qualifying licence. |
| Article 18 — right to exit with keys and data | **None.** Concerns source code, not user data. |

No Title II right is restricted.

## Impossibility-and-capture analysis

**What this makes impossible.** A closed, unauditable derivative of the reference implementation
operated as a public service. Under the current clause that is permitted; under the amendment it
is not.

**What this makes worth capturing.** Nothing new. The licence confers no authority, no revenue
and no privileged position. There is no licensing body, no exception-granting process and no
compliance surface — which is deliberate, because any of those would be a chokepoint under
Article 4.

**The honest counter-argument.** Copyleft is a restriction, and this project's instinct is to
prefer removing power over adding rules. The reply is that the restriction falls only on parties
who take the common work and withhold their improvements, and that the alternative is not
neutrality but a predictable transfer of advantage to whoever has the most capital.

## Centralisation analysis

Parties whose withdrawal would degrade the mechanism: **none.** Licensing requires no operator,
registry, authority or service.

The proposal's purpose is to reduce a centralisation risk (T8) rather than introduce one.

## Backwards compatibility

**No relicensing of existing code is possible, and this proposal does not attempt it.**

Contributions are accepted under a DCO with no contributor licence agreement, so copyright is
distributed across every contributor and no party holds the rights to relicense the corpus. That
is a deliberate anti-capture property and this proposal does not weaken it.

Consequently, ratification would apply to **new components and new contributions only**, with
existing MIT-licensed code either remaining MIT or being dual-licensed with its authors'
individual consent. A governance process capable of relicensing the whole corpus by vote would
itself be the capture vector this project exists to prevent.

**On independent implementations.** The concern that copyleft deters implementers is answered by
Article 44.4: the reference implementation is expressly not normative. An independent
implementation is written from the CC0 specifications, and Article 44.6 makes specification
sufficiency a conformance requirement. An organisation whose policy forbids copyleft can
implement VayuWeb without touching this code at all — which is the situation the specifications
were written to guarantee.

## Activation epoch

At least two epochs beyond the epoch in which this proposal reaches Accepted, per Article 47.3
and the epoch definition in [REGISTRY.md](REGISTRY.md).

Because this proposal changes no wire behaviour, the interval is not a deployment window but a
review window: a licensing change deserves the same waiting period as a protocol change, and
shortening it would set exactly the wrong precedent.
