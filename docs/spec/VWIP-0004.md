## Abstract

VayuWeb's corpus defined its namespace twice, a hundredfold apart, and this proposal settles it
at the larger figure.

Constitution Article 35.1 named eleven initial extensions. `NAMESPACE-CATALOGUE.md` enumerated
1,267, organised into 34 categories, and `NAMES.md` called that enumeration the launch set. An
implementer reading one document built a namespace a hundred times larger than one reading the
other, and each conformed to what they read.

This proposal amends Article 35.1 so the initial top-level domains are the **1,270 extensions of
the Namespace Annex** — the 1,267 catalogue entries plus `.p2p`, `.news` and `.blog`, which the
charter named but the catalogue omitted. It adds Articles 2.30 and 2.31 to define what the Annex
is and to require that a Node decide membership offline. It carries the collision review Article
35.6 demands, entry by entry. It does not open registration: ratification settles what the
namespace *is*, and Article 35.7's dormancy still governs when anything under it may be
registered.

## Motivation

### The disagreement had to be resolved, and downward was the wrong direction

A previous revision resolved this conflict by demoting all 1,267 entries to candidates a
verifier must reject. That was faithful to the charter as it then stood, and it was the wrong
answer, for a reason worth stating plainly: **eleven was never a decision.** No document in the
corpus argues for eleven. No analysis compares eleven to any other number. There is no threat
eleven mitigates and no cost it avoids. It reads exactly like what it is — a list somebody typed
early, which then acquired authority by being in the charter when the question was asked.

The catalogue, by contrast, is 1,267 entries carrying a stated purpose each, grouped into 34
categories, satisfying a published set of admission rules. Whatever else is true of it, it is
the artefact somebody thought about.

So the corpus contained a considered enumeration and an unconsidered one, and the unconsidered
one won because of where it sat. Deferring to the charter is normally correct — that hierarchy
is the whole reason a written constitution is worth having — but deference is for **resolving
ambiguity**, not for laundering an arbitrary number into a decision nobody made. Where the
charter is genuinely unclear, an implementer must not decide. Where the charter is clear and
wrong, an implementer must not decide *either*, and the remedy the charter itself provides is
this document.

### Why breadth is the right answer and not merely the larger one

On the clearnet, a top-level domain cost roughly USD 185,000 to apply for in the 2012 round,
plus about USD 25,000 a year to keep, in a window that has opened roughly once a decade. That
price is not a technical necessity — it is the cost of an authority: evaluation panels,
objection procedures, contractual compliance and the organisation that runs them. Its effect is
that the shape of the clearnet namespace is a record of who could afford the fee.

VayuWeb has no authority to fund and no fee to charge (Article 7 forbids one and Article 9.5
entrenches the prohibition). An extension therefore costs a proposal, and a registration under
it costs work. In that setting, restricting the initial set to eleven produces exactly one
effect: it concentrates every registration into eleven namespaces and makes the labels under
them scarce. **There is no party that arrangement protects and one class it enriches** — whoever
registers early under the eleven. A design with no scarcity to ration should not be rationing.

The squatting argument runs the same way and is worth doing arithmetically. Under eleven
extensions, cornering the string `atlas` costs eleven registrations, renewed annually forever.
Under 1,270 it costs 1,270. Breadth is not neutral with respect to squatting; it is the single
most effective structural defence available, because it multiplies the cost of the only attack
proof-of-work does not price — acquiring *every* form of a desirable name.

### What breadth genuinely costs, stated before the benefits

Two costs are real and neither is dismissed here.

**Defensive registration.** ICANN's gTLD expansion produced a defensive registration tax: a
brand felt obliged to register its name across hundreds of extensions to stop someone passing
off as it. Breadth made that tax larger. The honest position is that VayuWeb reduces this
pressure without eliminating it — registration costs work rather than money, so a defensive
portfolio is priced in CPU and decays unless continuously funded, and Article 36 refuses to
operate a trademark forum, so there is no venue in which a defensive registration buys standing.
What VayuWeb cannot do is make anyone feel safe, and a party that would have registered 300
defensive names on the clearnet may well register 1,270 here. That is a real cost of this
proposal.

**Comprehension.** A user cannot hold 1,270 extensions in their head, and a name under an
extension they have never seen carries less recognition than one under an extension they have.
This is mitigated by nothing in the protocol. It is a genuine argument for a smaller namespace,
it was weighed, and it lost to the concentration argument above — but a reader should see that
it was weighed rather than discover later that it was not.

### Why the amendment route rather than an edit

Article 41 makes the VWIP process exclusive over namespace policy, and Article 35.1 is charter
text. An implementer who wants a different namespace and edits a specification to describe one
has not changed the namespace; they have created a second document that disagrees with the
charter, which is precisely the defect this proposal exists to repair. The corpus already
contains the counter-example: `NAMES.md` asserted 1,267 for some time and it changed nothing,
because `REGISTRY.md` and the verifier enforced eleven.

## Specification

### 1. Article 35.1 is replaced

The full replacement text is in the [Full replacement text](#full-replacement-text) section
below, as Article 58.1.a requires. In summary, the amended Article:

1.1 Defines the initial top-level domains as the 1,270 extensions enumerated in the Namespace
Annex, incorporated by reference and fixed at commencement by the canonical digest of Article
1.7.

1.2 States that the Annex is **enumerative and closed**: a string absent from it is not a
top-level domain and a conformant Node SHALL reject a Record bearing one.

1.3 Continues to name eleven extensions in the Article's own text — `.vayu`, `.p2p`, `.free`,
`.decent`, `.libre`, `.sov`, `.dao`, `.indie`, `.open`, `.news` and `.blog` — so the founding set
survives loss of the Annex, and states in 35.1.c that this confers no rank.

1.4 Adds 35.1.a and 35.1.b recording why breadth is the choice and why enumeration is what makes
breadth safe.

### 2. Articles 2.30 and 2.31 are added

2.1 **Article 2.30** defines the Namespace Annex as an instrument distinct from the primitives
Annex of Article 2.26, and disapplies Article 2.27 to it. The separation is load-bearing and the
reason is stated in the Article: the primitives Annex must be replaceable without an amendment
or the charter dies with SHA-2, and the Namespace Annex must not be, because an editable list of
valid extensions is a mechanism for deciding whose Name resolves — the power Article 6 vests in
a keypair and nowhere else.

2.2 **Article 2.31** requires a Node to determine TLD validity by membership of the Annex it
holds, computed offline, with no network access and no query to any party. An implementation
that fetches, subscribes to, syncs or derives the valid set at run time is non-conformant under
Article 4.

### 3. The Annex

3.1 `docs/spec/NAMESPACE-CATALOGUE.md` becomes the **Namespace Annex** and is normative. It
enumerates 1,270 extensions across 34 categories.

3.2 Every entry satisfies the TLD grammar of `NAMES.md` — `tld = ALPHA *11(ALPHA / DIGIT)`, two
to twelve characters. This was verified mechanically over all 1,270 rather than asserted;
`.p2p` carries the only digit in the Annex.

3.3 No entry is duplicated. This was also verified mechanically, and it is not a hypothetical
check: `.vayu` was once listed twice under two different descriptions, which made a list
introduced as "twelve" hold eleven distinct entries.

3.4 The Annex's per-section counts sum to its stated total, and its stated total equals its row
count. A count that disagrees with its own enumeration is how the previous defect went unnoticed.

### 4. Implementation requirements

4.1 An implementation MUST enforce membership of the Annex and MUST NOT accept any other string
as a TLD.

4.2 An implementation MUST NOT restate the Annex in prose. Every restatement is a copy that can
drift, and the restatements in this corpus did drift — `RESOLUTION.md` hard-coded the founding
strings inline, `NAMES.md` asserted the catalogue figure, `REGISTRY.md` described the set by a
count, and the verifier enforced its own list. Documents reference the Annex; they do not repeat it.

4.3 An implementation that compiles the Annex in — which Article 2.31 effectively requires —
MUST carry a mechanical check that the compiled copy matches the Annex. The reference
implementation generates `registry/src/namespace.generated.ts` with
`scripts/generate-namespace.py` and re-runs it with `--check` in CI, so editing either side alone
fails the build. An independent implementation needs an equivalent discipline; the requirement is
the property, not the script.

## Collision review

Article 35.6 requires a collision review "against existing VayuWeb TLDs and against legacy DNS
strings likely to confuse users". It was performed mechanically over all 1,270 entries. What
follows states the results, including the ones that argue against this proposal.

### Against existing VayuWeb extensions

No collision. All 1,270 entries are distinct, distinct after case folding, and every entry
satisfies the TLD grammar. Three extensions named in Article 35.1 — `.p2p`, `.news` and `.blog` —
were **absent** from the 1,267-entry catalogue and are added by this proposal, because ratifying
the catalogue alone would have deleted three namespaces the charter names.

### Against legacy DNS: two-letter extensions

The Annex contains 60 two-letter extensions. **35 of them share a string with an ISO 3166-1
alpha-2 code delegated as a ccTLD in the legacy root:**

```text
ad ai ar as at ax be by cc cd ci co cv dj do fm gg hr id in io is it me ml my
no pm pr py qa so to tv us
```

The remaining 25 do not:

```text
an db ex fx go hd hi if ip js kb of ok on op os rd ui up ux vr we wp xr yo
```

**Assessment: recorded, not disqualifying.** The collision is of strings, not of resolution. A
VayuWeb name is reached through the `vayu://` scheme and its own resolver, which never consults
DNS — `RESOLUTION.md` step 3 makes the absence of DNS fallback enforceable rather than
aspirational, so `atlas.io` under VayuWeb and `atlas.io` on the clearnet are two names in two
namespaces that never meet in a resolver. What the collision produces is **user confusion**: a
reader who sees `atlas.io` cannot tell from the string which network it belongs to.

That is a real harm and the mitigation is partial. It is also not specific to two-letter strings:
`atlas.blog` collides in exactly the same way, and `.blog` is named in the charter itself. A rule
excluding colliding strings would have to exclude `.blog` and `.news`, which the charter names,
and would still not prevent ICANN from delegating a colliding string tomorrow — a limitation
`NAMES.md` already states and which no VayuWeb rule can remove.

The countervailing consideration is that excluding the 35 would concede that a ccTLD delegation
made in the 1980s constrains what a network built in the 2020s may call things. Article 35.2
makes extensions flat and equal precisely so that no external authority's list governs this one.

### Against legacy DNS: generic strings

Five entries share a string with a widely known ICANN generic: `.blog`, `.news`, `.forum`,
`.physics` and `.tv` (which is also a ccTLD, counted above).

`.blog` and `.news` are named in the text of Article 35.1 and are retained for that reason; the
Annex's "no echo of a well-known ICANN generic" rule now states this carve-out explicitly rather
than claiming a property it does not have. `.forum` and `.physics` are new findings from this
review, recorded here rather than removed — removing them would apply a stricter rule to two
entries than the charter applies to itself.

**What this check does not prove.** It was run against a hand-maintained list of legacy and
widely known generic TLDs, not against the full ICANN root zone, which carries roughly 1,200
delegated generics. The 35 ccTLD collisions are complete, because ISO 3166-1 alpha-2 is a closed
and stable set. The generic-collision figure of five is a **floor, not a total**, and a reviewer
with the current root zone to hand should expect to find more. Presenting five as exhaustive
would be the kind of claim Article 21 exists to forbid.

## Security considerations

**A larger namespace is not a larger attack surface at the protocol layer.** Validity is
membership of a compiled-in set; the cost of checking 1,270 entries and eleven is the same hash
lookup, and no code path branches on the size of the set.

**The genuine risk this proposal introduces is copy drift.** The Annex now has to reach every
implementation, and an implementation whose copy is wrong by one entry accepts names others
reject — a namespace fork that presents as an intermittent resolution failure rather than as an
error, which makes it expensive to diagnose. Section 4.3 requires a mechanical check, and the
reference implementation carries two independent ones: the generator's `--check` mode, and a
test that reads the Annex back and compares it to the compiled set. Both were mutation-tested —
the generated file was edited by one entry and each detected it, with the entry named.

**Homograph risk rises with the number of extensions,** because more strings mean more
opportunities for a confusable pair. Article 35.4 answers this mechanically — confusable-script
mixing is rejected at validation — and labels are ASCII-only at launch, which removes the
mixed-script case entirely for v1. The residual risk is ASCII look-alikes across extensions
(`.rn` against `.m`, say), and it is not fully mitigated. It is bounded by the fact that no
single-character extension exists.

**What this makes newly attractive to an attacker: nothing that was not already.** The Annex is
a static list in a signed, content-addressed document. There is no fetch to intercept, no
authority to compel, and no run-time decision to influence. That is a direct consequence of
Article 2.31, and it is the main reason the Annex is closed rather than derived.

## Privacy considerations

**Neutral at the protocol layer, with one improvement.** Because Article 2.31 requires the valid
set to be decided offline, a Node reveals nothing by checking a TLD. A design that fetched or
subscribed to the namespace would leak which extensions a user is interested in — and therefore a
coarse profile of what they browse — to whoever serves the list. Closing the Annex removes that
channel entirely rather than mitigating it, which is Article 5's preference for impossibility
over policy.

**A second-order effect, stated because it is real.** With 1,270 extensions, the extension a
name sits under is more informative about its holder than it would be with eleven: registering
under `.hijra` or `.zakat` or `.mutual` discloses something a `.vayu` registration does not. This
is a property of a descriptive namespace, not of this proposal — the same is true of the
clearnet — and it is a user's choice to make. It is noted so a registrant can make it knowingly,
and `.vayu` remains available as a general-purpose extension that discloses nothing.

**No new data is collected, retained or transmitted by this change.** There is nothing to
retain: the Annex is static.

## Rights-impact analysis

| Title II Article | Effect |
|---|---|
| Article 10 — right to register without permission | **Expansion.** The set of names registrable without anyone's approval grows by a factor of about 115. No new permission, approval or eligibility condition is introduced. |
| Article 11 — right to hold, renew and not be revoked | **None.** No existing tenure is altered. The amendment is prospective and there are no live names at commencement, so no holder can be affected in either direction. |
| Article 12 — right to publish and to be reachable | **None.** Publication and reachability are unchanged; only the set of valid `tld` values grows. |
| Article 16 — right to interoperate and implement independently | **Expansion, with a cost.** Expansion because the Annex is a single enumerated source an independent implementer can copy exactly, replacing four disagreeing restatements. The cost is that they must now copy 1,270 entries correctly rather than eleven, which section 4.3 addresses by requiring a mechanical check rather than care. |
| Article 17 — right to fork | **None.** Nothing here conditions, delays or penalises a fork. A fork may adopt a different Annex. |
| Article 20 — freedom from retroactive rule changes | **None.** The activation epoch is in the future and no Record exists to be revalidated. |

No Title II right is restricted by this proposal.

**Entrenchment check (Article 9).** Article 35 is not entrenched. The entrenched list at
Articles 9.2–9.16 does not include the namespace enumeration, and this proposal touches no
entrenched substance: it creates no party whose cooperation is required (9.2), no recovery or
override capability (9.4), no fee (9.5), no permission requirement (9.6), and no revocation
power (9.7). It **strengthens** 9.6 by enlarging what may be registered without permission.
Article 9.19 requires this to be checked by effect and not only by wording, and the check by
effect is that a larger permissionless namespace cannot make registration more permissioned.

## Impossibility-and-capture analysis

**What this makes impossible.** A namespace that changes without a ratified proposal. By closing
the Annex and forbidding run-time derivation (Article 2.31), there is no mechanism — no feed, no
quorum, no log entry, no operator — by which the set of valid extensions can change on a running
Node. Someone who wants a new extension has one route, and it takes 30 + 90 + 180 days.

**What this makes worth capturing.** Editorial control of the Annex, which is a real prize and
larger than it was: the party who decides what goes in decides what can be registered. Three
things reduce it. It cannot be exercised quietly, because the Annex is part of the
content-addressed canonical text and a change moves the digest. It cannot be exercised quickly,
because Article 35.6's dormancy is measured in half-years. And it is worth less than it looks,
because Article 35.8 forbids reserved names, sunrise periods and pre-allocation, so whoever adds
an extension gains no claim to anything under it — they gain, in the charter's words, "exactly
one thing: the fact of having proposed".

**The honest residual.** Proposing 1,270 extensions at once is itself an exercise of that
influence, made by one author, at the one moment in the project's life when the amendment cost is
lowest. That is a fair objection and it is entered in the Objection Register below rather than
answered away. The mitigations are that the enumeration was not written for this proposal, that
the proposer gains no name under any of it, and that the alternative — eleven — was an exercise
of the same influence by the same author with less analysis attached.

## Centralisation analysis

Article 4 requires identifying every party whose withdrawal would degrade the mechanism.

| Party | Effect of withdrawal |
|---|---|
| The Annex's author | **None after ratification.** The enumeration is a static list in the canonical text. Nothing about validating a TLD consults, contacts or depends on whoever wrote it. |
| The editorial panel | **None on existing extensions; delays new ones.** Article 46.4 suspends editorial transitions when the panel falls below three, which would block new Naming VWIPs. It cannot invalidate a ratified extension or a registered name. |
| The reference implementation's maintainers | **None.** Article 44.4 makes the reference implementation non-normative. An independent implementation reads the Annex, not the generated module. |
| Any registry, mirror, index or gateway | **None.** No party is consulted at validation time; Article 2.31 forbids it. |
| ICANN or any DNS operator | **None, in either direction.** VayuWeb does not consult the root zone, and cannot prevent a colliding delegation. See the collision review. |

**The list is not empty, and the honest reading of it** is that the only dependency of substance
is on the editorial panel, and only for *future* additions. Article 46 constrains that panel six
ways — three-person minimum, staggered fixed terms, a one-third employer cap, mandatory recusal,
no merit veto, a 120-day dead-man rule — and this proposal adds no power to it. Section 3.2 of
VWIP-0000 warns that an analysis identifying no dependent party at all should be treated as
unfinished; this one identifies the panel, and does not claim the dependency is zero.

## Migration and rollback

**Migration is empty by construction.** There are no live names, no deployed implementations and
no registry at commencement, so nothing migrates. Every implementation regenerates its compiled
set from the Annex and its checks pass or fail mechanically.

**Rollback is available and is not symmetrical, which is the point.** Before any name is
registered, this amendment can be reversed by a further amendment at the same cost. After names
exist under an extension, it cannot: Article 35.9 forbids deleting a TLD with live names — the
only available action is TLD-FREEZE, under which existing names continue to resolve, renew,
transfer, delegate and publish indefinitely, and Article 35.10 makes TLD-RETIRE reachable only
when no live names remain or every holder has migrated by their own signed action under a path
open for at least five years.

So the practical rollback window closes with the first registration under a contested extension,
and the honest statement of the risk is: **an extension that turns out to be a mistake is a
mistake VayuWeb keeps.** That asymmetry is deliberate — it is what makes Article 11's promise
that a Name is not revocable mean anything — and it is the strongest argument available for
ratifying a smaller set. It is answered by the fact that Article 35.9 makes an unused extension
harmless: an extension nobody registers under is an unused row in a list, costing a hash-set
entry, and Article 35.2 forbids any client from presenting one as more official than another.

## Activation epoch

**Strictly in the future, per Article 47, and not fixed by this document.**

The amendment takes effect on publication of a new canonical anchor under Article 1.8 (Article
58.6). Registration under any extension is separately gated by Article 35.7: the activation epoch
must be published not less than 180 days in advance, and Article 35.6 requires not less than 180
days of dormancy between ratification and availability.

The epoch is deliberately left unset here. Article 35.7's purpose is that advance knowledge
confers no landrush advantage, and naming a date in the proposal that creates the extensions
would hand the proposer and every close reader a 180-day head start on preparing registrations —
which is the precise advantage the clause exists to deny. The epoch is published separately, once
a registry exists to receive registrations.

**Nothing in this proposal makes any name registrable.** It settles what the namespace is.

## Expiry of transitional mechanisms

**This proposal introduces no transitional mechanism, and that is a deliberate refusal.**

Article 48.9 records why: temporary scaffolding otherwise becomes permanent. The obvious
scaffolding — a phased rollout in which some extensions open before others — is rejected because
a phase order is a ranking, and Article 35.2 forbids ranking extensions. Every extension in the
Annex becomes registrable at the same epoch or the flat-and-equal rule is a claim the design does
not honour.

One drafting-stage distinction expires on its own and is recorded here because it is
load-bearing for this proposal's admissibility. VWIP-0000 §2.4.b states that before commencement
under Article 60.3 there is no charter in force to amend, so changes in that window are drafting
rather than amendment and Article 58's twelve-month machinery does not reach them. **That window
is open now**, because the canonical digest required by Articles 1.7 and 1.9 has not been
published — which is itself a defect recorded separately. It closes permanently at commencement,
with no possibility of renewal, and after it closes a proposal of this kind takes twelve months,
two readings, a concurrent 75 percent supermajority in four constituencies, and double
ratification.

That is stated in the open rather than relied on quietly, because it is the single most
attackable claim in this document: an author asserting that the rules do not yet apply to the
change they want is exactly the move a reader should distrust. Two things constrain it. It is
self-extinguishing and nobody controls when it ends — commencement is triggered by publishing an
anchor, not by anyone's discretion. And this proposal is written to the full Article 58 standard
anyway: full replacement text, rights-impact analysis, capture analysis, prospective-only effect.
What is missing is the ratification arithmetic, which no one could have performed because the
constituencies of Article 40 do not yet exist. What is not missing is the reasoning, which anyone
can audit.

the conformance suite:

| `tld = "zine"` | accepted (last entry of the Annex in sort order) |

## Full replacement text

Article 35.1, as amended, reads in full:

> 35.1 The initial top-level domains are the one thousand two hundred and seventy extensions
> enumerated in the Namespace Annex, which is incorporated into this Article by reference and
> fixed at commencement by the canonical digest of Article 1.7. The Annex is enumerative and
> closed: a string absent from it is not a top-level domain, and a conformant Node SHALL reject a
> Record bearing one. Eleven are named here so that the founding set survives loss of the Annex:
> .vayu, .p2p, .free, .decent, .libre, .sov, .dao, .indie, .open, .news and .blog.
>
> 35.1.a Breadth at launch is a deliberate choice against a scarcity the clearnet manufactures. A
> top-level domain there cost roughly USD 185,000 to apply for in the 2012 round plus about USD
> 25,000 a year to keep, in an application window that has opened roughly once a decade — a price
> that selects for capital rather than for use, and that makes the shape of the namespace a
> consequence of who could afford the fee. Here an extension costs a proposal and the
> registrations under it cost work, so there is no mechanism by which restricting the initial set
> to eleven would benefit anyone except the holders of those eleven. Where a design has no
> scarcity to ration, rationing is not prudence.
>
> 35.1.b Enumeration is not a limit on breadth; it is what makes breadth safe. A set derived at

> 2.30 The **Namespace Annex** is a distinct instrument from the primitives Annex of 2.26, and

continues to govern the creation of any extension beyond the Annex.

## Objection Register

Article 43.6 requires every unresolved objection to be entered permanently, with the reasoning by
which it was overruled and the identity of whoever recorded that reasoning. These were raised
during drafting, by the author against the author's own proposal. They are entered as unresolved
rather than answered, because a Register that only contains objections the proposer found easy is
not a Register.

**O-1. The proposer is exploiting the pre-commencement window.** This amendment would take twelve
months, four constituencies and double ratification if proposed a week after the anchor is
published. Proposing it now, by the same person who wrote both conflicting enumerations, is
availing oneself of the cheapest possible moment.
*Recorded by: Ankush Choudhary Johal. Overruled, partially.* The window is real, self-
extinguishing, and controlled by nobody — but the objection is correct that it is being used, and
the honest answer is that the alternative is worse: leaving a hundredfold contradiction in the
corpus until it can be fixed expensively serves no one. Not overruled: the observation that the
same author wrote both sides. That is a fact about this project's bus factor, it is exactly what
Article 53's concentration metrics exist to surface, and it is not fixed by this proposal.

**O-2. 1,270 is as arbitrary as eleven.** The proposal's central charge against eleven is that
nobody argued for it. Nobody argued for 1,270 either — it is the length of a list somebody
happened to write.
*Recorded by: Ankush Choudhary Johal. Not overruled.* This is correct and is not answered here.
The defence offered is narrower than the objection: 1,270 is not argued to be the right number,
only to be *better reasoned* than eleven, because each entry carries a stated purpose and a
category and satisfies published admission rules. A namespace whose size was actually derived
from something would be better than both, and no such derivation is offered.

**O-3. Ratifying 1,270 extensions in one instrument defeats Article 35.6's per-extension
scrutiny.** The Article contemplates a collision review, an objection window and a dormancy *per
extension*. Doing 1,270 at once gives each entry a 1,270th of the attention the Article
envisages.
*Recorded by: Ankush Choudhary Johal. Overruled, with the reasoning stated.* Article 35.6 governs
extensions coming into being **after** the initial set; Article 35.1 owns the initial set and has
always enumerated it in one instrument. Eleven at once was equally a batch. But the objection
identifies something real — per-entry scrutiny genuinely is thinner here than it would be for a
single later proposal — and the mitigation is only that the collision review in this document was
run mechanically over every entry rather than sampled.
