## Abstract

VayuWeb can name a site and it cannot fetch one from anybody else. A name resolves to a content
identifier, the resolver knows how to verify every block that arrives against the identifier that
referred it, and there is no protocol for asking another machine for those blocks. A site is
therefore readable only on the machine that imported it, which is a parallel web with one reader.

This proposal defines that protocol: four messages over the same transport contract replication
already uses, carrying content-addressed blocks between peers that know nothing about each other.
It is deliberately smaller than the block-exchange protocols in general use, and the omissions are
the substance of the design rather than an unfinished edge of it. There is **no advertisement of
what a peer holds**, **no reputation score**, **no debt ratio**, and **no distinction on the wire
between a peer that lacks a block and one that declines to send it**. Each of those is a feature
elsewhere, and each of them either discloses a reader's interests, or creates a standing that a
peer can be denied.

What makes the protocol safe is not that peers behave. It is that the receiver verifies every
block against the identifier that referred it before the block is used for anything, so the worst
a hostile peer achieves is to waste bandwidth within a stated budget.

## Motivation

### The gap is not a missing optimisation

[HOSTING.md](HOSTING.md) specifies how a directory becomes a content identifier, in byte-exact
detail, and it has been checked against published reference identifiers rather than only against
itself. [RESOLUTION.md](RESOLUTION.md) clauses 12.1 to 12.3 specify how an arriving tree of blocks
is verified: recursively, against the referring identifier at each step, under a bound on blocks
and depth rather than on assembled bytes. `registry/src/fetch.ts` implements that traversal, and
it takes its blocks from a `BlockSource` the caller supplies.

Nothing supplies that source from another machine. The publish path and the verify path are both
built and they are joined by nothing, which is why the roadmap's Phase 4 acceptance test — a site
published on one machine, fetched and rendered by a second that was never told where it came from
— is not merely unpassed but unattemptable.

### Why not simply use bitswap

Bitswap is the obvious answer and it is close to the right one. Adopting it unchanged would be
faster than writing this, and there is a real interoperability argument for it: VayuWeb already
chose sha2-256 over the faster BLAKE2b for content hashing precisely so that ordinary IPFS nodes
can serve VayuWeb sites.

Three properties stop it being adopted as-is, and they are properties this corpus has already
committed against elsewhere.

**A ledger is a reputation score.** Bitswap implementations keep a per-peer accounting of bytes
sent and received and use it to decide whom to serve. [REPLICATION.md](REPLICATION.md) 1.4 lists
"a peer reputation score" among the things replication deliberately does not have, and the reason
generalises exactly: a score is a standing, a standing can be denied, and a mechanism that can
deny standing is a mechanism worth capturing. Constitution Article 4 forbids making any party's
availability load-bearing; a scored peer set makes *every* peer's history load-bearing on its
future access.

**`HAVE` advertisement is disclosure with no compensating benefit to the discloser.**
[CONTENT-SECURITY.md](CONTENT-SECURITY.md) 5.6 names the exposure: "DHT and bitswap traffic reveal
which blocks a reader wants". That half is unavoidable — you cannot ask for a block without naming
it. The other half is avoidable. A peer that answers `HAVE` to a probe has told a stranger what it
is hosting, and in a hostile jurisdiction "this machine holds that site" may be the only fact an
adversary needs. Asking is a cost the reader chooses; answering an inventory query is a cost the
host did not.

**A distinguishable "I don't have it" is a probe oracle.** If a peer's refusal is distinguishable
from its absence, then an adversary who cannot get a block can still enumerate what a peer holds
by asking. Making the two identical costs the protocol a small amount of efficiency and removes
the oracle entirely.

An implementation MAY still fetch VayuWeb content over bitswap from an ordinary IPFS node, and
SHOULD, because the identifiers are deliberately compatible. What it MUST NOT do is treat that
path as satisfying this specification: the properties above are the specification.

### Why the verification rules are not restated here

They are in RESOLUTION.md 12.1 to 12.3 and this document does not repeat them. A second copy of a
verification rule is a copy that drifts, and this corpus has produced that failure often enough
that the discipline is worth the cross-reference. What this document adds is the wire, and the
consequences on the wire of a verification failure — which is a different question from what
verification is.

## Specification

The key words MUST, MUST NOT, SHALL, SHALL NOT, SHOULD, SHOULD NOT and MAY are to be interpreted
as described in RFC 2119, under the usage discipline of Constitution Article 3.

### 1. Scope

1.1 This protocol transports **blocks**: octet strings addressed by the content identifiers
[HOSTING.md](HOSTING.md) defines. It decides nothing about what a block means, what site it
belongs to, or whether it should be kept.

1.2 This protocol MUST NOT be used to transport registry records. Records have their own protocol
in [REPLICATION.md](REPLICATION.md), a different verification rule, and a different failure
model. A single connection MAY carry both, but the message sets are disjoint and a receiver MUST
NOT accept a record over a block-exchange message or a block over a replication message.

1.3 The requester decides what to ask for. This protocol has no notion of a subscription, a push,
or a peer volunteering content that was not requested.

### 2. Transport binding

2.1 The protocol runs over any channel providing **ordered, reliable, framed, bidirectional**
delivery of octet strings — the same contract [REPLICATION.md](REPLICATION.md) 2.1 states, for
the same reason. It does not require confidentiality, authentication of the remote party, or a
stable peer identity.

2.2 The reference binding is Hyperswarm over HyperDHT, discovering peers on the topic
`BLAKE2b-256("VayuWeb-BlockExchange-v1")`. **This binding is not normative**, per Article 4: a
protocol defined in terms of one discovery network makes that network's operators load-bearing.

2.3 An implementation MUST NOT treat the transport's authentication, if any, as evidence about a
block. A block's authority is that it hashes to the identifier that referred it. Nothing about the
channel adds to that, and an implementation that skips verification for a "known" peer has removed
the only check there is.

2.4 A peer SHOULD use a distinct transport identity per session, and MUST use one per Private Mode
launch, per CONTENT-SECURITY.md 5.6. A stable identity across sessions links a reader's requests
into a profile that no message-level rule here can unlink.

### 3. Messages

3.1 Every message is a deterministic CBOR map per [REGISTRY.md](REGISTRY.md), carrying a text key
`t` naming its type. A message whose encoding is not the canonical encoding of its own content
MUST be rejected.

3.2 A receiver MUST reject an unknown `t` by ignoring the message, and MUST NOT close the
connection for that reason alone.

| `t` | Direction | Payload |
|---|---|---|
| `BHELLO` | both, first | `v` uint protocol version · `max` uint largest block this peer will accept, in bytes |
| `BWANT` | either | `cids` array of bstr, each a binary CIDv1 |
| `BLOCKS` | reply to `BWANT` | `blks` array of bstr, each the octets of one block |
| `BDONE` | reply to `BWANT` | `cids` array of bstr, the requested identifiers not being answered |

3.3 A peer MUST send `BHELLO` before any other message and MUST NOT send `BWANT` before receiving
the remote `BHELLO`. `v` is `1` for this specification. A peer receiving a `v` it does not
implement MUST close the connection; unlike an unknown message type, an unknown protocol version
is not something the rest of the session can be interpreted around.

3.4 `BHELLO.max` is a **statement of what the sender will accept**, not a request. A peer MUST NOT
send a block larger than the remote's declared `max`, and MUST NOT allocate anything on the basis
of a remote `max` — see 5.1.

3.4.a A peer MUST NOT declare a `max` above the block limit in section 5, and a receiver MUST
enforce that limit **regardless of any `max` either peer has declared**. Read without this clause,
3.4 makes the advertisement the governing bound, and an implementer following it accepts a ten-
megabyte block from a peer that declared ten megabytes. That would let a stranger raise the
receiver's own limit by asking it to, which is the one thing a limit must never be reachable by.
A declared `max` can only ever be *lower* than the protocol's, and lowering it harms nobody but
the peer that declared it.

3.5 `BLOCKS.blks` carries blocks **without their identifiers**. The receiver computes the
identifier from the octets, which it must do in any case, and a declared identifier alongside the
bytes would be a field that is either redundant or a lie. This is the same reasoning that keeps
UnixFS declared sizes from being treated as authority.

3.6 A `BLOCKS` message MAY answer a `BWANT` partially and in any order. Blocks are matched to
requests by their computed identifier, never by position.

3.6.a A `BWANT` MUST NOT name the same identifier twice, and a peer **MUST NOT have the same
identifier outstanding in more than one `BWANT`** on a connection. Sixty-four copies of one
identifier is a request for one block and a demand for sixty-four, inside a message that passes
every limit in section 5. A receiver MAY refuse a `BWANT` containing a repeat, and MAY refuse one
naming an identifier it is already holding a request for.

The second half was missing from the first version of this clause, which said only "a `BWANT`
MUST NOT name the same identifier twice". Section 5 permits **eight outstanding `BWANT`s per
connection**, so the same attack ran again across eight messages instead of one array — smaller by
a factor of eight, and equally free. The clause had been written from the example that prompted it
rather than from the property it was protecting.

3.7 A peer that will not answer part of a `BWANT` **MUST** send `BDONE` naming those identifiers.
`BDONE` carries no reason. See 6.2.

### 4. Verification and failure

4.1 A receiver MUST compute the identifier of every arriving block and MUST discard any block
whose identifier it did not request. Verification against the *referring* identifier during
traversal is specified by [RESOLUTION.md](RESOLUTION.md) 12.1 and is unchanged by this document.

4.2 A block arriving with an identifier the receiver did not request is not an error to report; it
is discarded silently and counted against the connection's budget under section 5. A peer MAY be
disconnected for repeating it. Reporting it back would confirm to the sender what the receiver was
and was not expecting.

4.3 **A block that fails verification is an attack, not an unavailability.** The receiver MUST NOT
use the bytes for any purpose, MUST NOT cache them, and MUST NOT ask that peer for that identifier
again on that connection.

4.4 **Asking a different peer for the same identifier after a verification failure is permitted
and expected.** This is not the fallback RESOLUTION.md forbids, and the distinction is worth
stating because it is easy to get backwards. RESOLUTION.md's rule forbids falling back to a
*different content source* — a different record entry, a different name — after an integrity
failure, because the failure is evidence about the source. Here the identifier is unchanged, the
verification is unchanged, and only the counterparty differs. A protocol in which one hostile peer
can make a site permanently unfetchable would hand every hostile peer a veto, which is precisely
the outcome Article 4 exists to prevent.

4.5 A peer that sends no answer at all is not in violation. There is no liveness obligation in
this protocol, and inventing one creates a duty that can be failed and therefore enforced —
REPLICATION.md 5.2 states the same rule for the same reason. A requester bounds its own wait; it
does not accuse.

4.5.a Because 4.5 holds, a requester **MUST bound how long a `BWANT` stays outstanding** and MUST
release the slot when that bound elapses. The absence of a duty on one side has to be paid for by
a bound on the other, and without this clause the two rules combine into a deadlock that is free
to cause: eight slots per connection, no obligation to answer, and no requirement ever to give up.
A peer that greets and then says nothing takes every slot permanently while breaking no rule. The
bound is the requester's to choose; having one is not.

### 5. Limits

Every limit below MUST be enforced. Section 2.3 leaves resource exhaustion as the only attack a
hostile peer retains, and an unbounded field is an invitation.

| Quantity | Limit | Why this number |
|---|---|---|
| Message encoding | 1,114,112 bytes | One maximum block plus CBOR framing and nothing more. |
| Block octets | 1,048,576 bytes | Four times the 262,144-byte chunk size, leaving room for a directory node with the maximum link count while refusing anything that could only be an amplification attempt. |
| `BWANT.cids` length | 64 | Bounds the work one message can ask for. A syncing peer sends many `BWANT`s rather than one large one. |
| `BLOCKS.blks` length | 64 | Matches `BWANT.cids`, so an honest reply is never split for a reason the requester cannot predict. |
| Each identifier | 64 bytes | The `cid` entry bound from REGISTRY.md, restated so a receiver checks it before decoding rather than after. |
| Outstanding `BWANT`s per connection | 8 | Bounds memory held for in-flight requests. |
| Unrequested blocks tolerated per connection | 16 | A peer that keeps sending what nobody asked for is spending the receiver's bandwidth on the receiver's behalf. |

5.1 A peer MUST NOT allocate memory proportional to a value a remote peer asserted. `BHELLO.max`
is a claim, not a measurement: a peer declaring a maximum of 2^53 MUST cost the receiver nothing
beyond the message itself. Allocate for what has arrived, never for what was announced.

5.2 A requester MUST bound the total number of blocks it will accept for one logical fetch, and
MUST bound the depth it will traverse, per RESOLUTION.md 12.2. Those bounds belong to the
resolver, not to this protocol, and this protocol MUST NOT be relied upon to supply them.

5.3 A peer exceeding a limit MAY be disconnected.

### 6. What this protocol deliberately does not have

6.1 **No advertisement.** There is no message by which a peer states what it holds, and a peer
MUST NOT answer a `BWANT` for an identifier with anything other than the block or a `BDONE`. The
inventory of what a machine hosts is disclosed by the machine's owner, deliberately, or not at
all.

6.2 **No reason on `BDONE`.** A peer that lacks a block and a peer that declines to send one MUST
emit the identical message. A distinguishable refusal is an oracle for enumerating what a peer
holds, which is 6.1 defeated by a side channel.

6.2.a **The obligation is on the sender, and deliberately so.** An earlier draft of this clause
said a *receiver* MUST NOT attempt to distinguish the two cases by timing or ordering. That is
unenforceable and points at the wrong party: nothing stops a receiver measuring, and a rule only
the honest follow is not a rule. What is enforceable is the sender behaving identically, so the
duty is written there.

6.2.b For the same reason 3.7 makes `BDONE` a MUST rather than a SHOULD. If answering were
optional then *whether* a peer answers would itself be the signal — a peer that reports the blocks
it lacks and stays silent on the ones it declines has defeated 6.2 without ever sending a
distinguishable message. An optional message is a channel.

6.2.c **Content is one of four observables, and all four are constrained.** 6.2 as first written
required the identical *message* and stopped there. A receiver also sees how many messages arrive,
in what order the identifiers appear, and when — and each is enough on its own. A peer that emits
one `BDONE` per identifier it lacks and one combined `BDONE` for the ones it refuses has sent
byte-identical messages and answered the question anyway. So has one that lists the refused
identifiers last. Therefore:

- A peer MUST send **exactly one `BDONE`** in answer to one `BWANT`, naming every identifier it is
  not answering, or none at all if it is answering all of them.
- The identifiers in `BDONE.cids` MUST appear in the **same order as the `BWANT`** that prompted
  them. That order is a permutation the requester chose, so it carries nothing about the peer.
  Sorting by the identifier bytes would also be deterministic, and would be a permutation of the
  peer's choosing — deterministic is not the same as uninformative.
- The answer MUST be decided in full before any of it is sent, and **MUST NOT be emitted
  incrementally** as the peer works through its store and its policy. Timing is the observable no
  message format can constrain, and this is the rule that closes it: a peer that consults the
  blockstore, then the policy, then emits, spends the same time in the same order for every
  request.

`blockDoneFor` in `registry/src/blockx.ts` closes the first two by construction. The third is a
rule about control flow and no function signature can enforce it, which is stated here rather than
implied.

6.3 **No ledger, no debt ratio, no reputation.** A peer's history MUST NOT affect whether it is
served. This admits free-riding and that is an accepted cost, stated rather than hidden: the
alternative is a standing that can be denied, and a mechanism that can deny standing is a
mechanism worth capturing. What bounds abuse is section 5, applied identically to every peer.

6.4 **No availability figure.** This protocol produces no count of how many peers hold a block and
no percentage of a site that is "available". Constitution Article 23 forbids the figure, and
[HOSTING.md](HOSTING.md)'s availability rules already hold the line that silence is reported as
silence rather than as absence.

6.5 **No cancellation.** Bitswap has `CANCEL`; this does not. A cancellation tells a peer that the
block arrived from somewhere else, which is a disclosure about the requester's other connections.

The cost is real and is not waved away: a requester that obtained a block elsewhere still
**receives a copy it no longer needs**, and discards it on arrival. That is bandwidth spent for a
privacy gain — a trade, not a free win. It is accepted because the wasted transfer is bounded by
section 5 and paid by the requester, while the disclosure would be unbounded in time and paid by
somebody who never chose it.

### 7. Conformance

Each of the following is an executable test asserting on observed behaviour.

7.1 A peer that sends a block whose octets do not hash to a requested identifier causes the
receiver to discard the block, to leave its state unchanged, and to not request that identifier
from that peer again on that connection.

7.2 A peer that sends a block nobody requested causes no state change, and the sixteenth such
block within one connection is the last that is tolerated.

7.3 A `BHELLO` declaring `max` of 2^53 does not cause the receiver to allocate.

7.4 A `BWANT` for 65 identifiers is refused at decode, without iterating the array.

7.5 A block one octet over the block limit is refused before its identifier is computed.

7.6 Two receivers given the same blocks in different orders reach the same result, and a receiver
given a bad block followed by the correct one for the same identifier reaches the same result as
one given only the correct one.

7.7 A `BDONE` for an identifier a peer is known to hold is byte-identical to a `BDONE` for one it
does not.

7.8 A `BLOCKS` carrying a block above the section 5 limit is refused by a receiver that has itself
declared a larger `max`, and by one whose remote declared a larger `max`. Both directions, because
3.4.a is about which bound wins and an implementation can get one right and the other wrong.

7.9 A peer that receives a `BWANT` it will not fully answer emits a `BDONE`. A run in which the
peer holds none of the requested blocks and one in which it holds all of them but serves none
produce the same sequence of messages.

7.10 A requester whose peer greets and then sends nothing releases its `BWANT` slots within its
own stated bound, and remains able to issue new requests afterwards.

7.11 A `BWANT` naming one identifier twice is either refused or answered once. It is never
answered twice, and neither is an identifier named in two `BWANT`s outstanding at the same time.

7.12 A peer that lacks every requested block and a peer that holds every one of them but serves
none produce the same number of messages, carrying the same bytes, with the identifiers in the
same order.

## Security considerations

**The threat this protocol accepts.** A peer can waste the receiver's bandwidth and CPU, up to the
budget in section 5, by sending wrong blocks, unrequested blocks, or nothing. This is the residual
attack after 2.3, it is bounded, and bounding it is what section 5 is for.

**The threat it closes.** Substituting content is closed by verification, and closed at every
depth rather than only at the root — which was a real defect in RESOLUTION.md before clauses 12.1
to 12.3 were written, and the one substitution a reader could never notice, since the name, the
record and the root are all genuine.

**Amplification.** A block-exchange protocol is a natural amplifier: a small request names a large
response.

The smallest request that elicits the largest reply is a `BWANT` naming one 36-byte identifier —
**53 bytes** on the wire. The largest honest reply to it is one block at the block limit, which
encodes to 1,048,597 bytes. One message's leverage is therefore roughly **19,784×**, and 21,020×
measured against the whole-message bound.

That figure has been wrong twice, in the same direction, and recording how is more useful than the
number itself.

The first version said the block limit and the `BWANT.cids` limit "together cap one message's
leverage at 64 megabytes" — 64 blocks at 1 MiB each. But 64 MiB does not fit in a 1,114,112-byte
message, so that reply cannot exist: the two bounds interact and the binding one is the message
size, which makes `BLOCKS.blks` a bound on array iteration rather than on volume. Wrong by 64×.

The correction was **272×**, and it was wrong too, for a different and worse reason: it divided the
largest reply by the *largest* request. Amplification is max(response) over **min**(request), so
dividing by the biggest possible request yields the smallest ratio — the opposite of a cap, two
sentences after this section opens "a small request names a large response". Wrong by another 73×,
and written by the pass that was fixing the first error.

Both flattered the protocol. For scale, this makes VayuWeb's block exchange a considerably better
reflector than the classic amplifiers, and an implementation sizing per-connection egress budgets
from 272× would set them two orders of magnitude too loose. The divisor is now the *measured*
encoding of the minimal request rather than a product of two limits, and a test recomputes the
published figure from the codec on every run.

Per *message* is not per connection. Nothing here bounds how many `BLOCKS` messages a peer sends,
and nothing here should: the requester's own block and depth budget under 5.2 is what caps a
logical fetch, and it belongs to the resolver because only the resolver knows what it asked for.
Both bounds are required and neither is sufficient alone.

**Disconnection as a weapon.** Section 5.3 permits disconnecting a peer that exceeds a limit,
which means a peer can get itself disconnected on purpose. That is not an attack on anyone but
itself: there is no membership list to be evicted from and no state that survives the connection.

**What a passive observer learns.** Which identifiers a peer requested, and therefore which sites
it is reading. This is not closed by anything here and MUST NOT be claimed as closed. Section 2.4
and the atomic-DAG fetch of CONTENT-SECURITY.md 5.6 reduce the granularity from "which page" to
"which site"; volume and timing remain, and remain uncloseable.

**Why "none" would have been wrong.** An earlier framing of this section said the protocol has no
security consequence because verification happens above it. That is false in the way that matters:
verification decides whether bytes are *used*, and every resource this protocol spends is spent
before that decision is reached.

## Privacy considerations

**A request discloses interest, irreducibly.** Naming a block is how you ask for it. The
disclosure is to whoever is asked, and to anyone observing the channel.

**What is reduced.** Fetching and verifying an entire site DAG atomically on first request, per
CONTENT-SECURITY.md 5.6, means path-level block selection cannot be used as an exfiltration
oracle: a reader who opens one page of a site requests the same blocks as one who opens another.
Rotating the transport identity per session unlinks sessions from each other. Preferring already
connected peers and locally pinned blocks reduces how many parties see the request at all.

**What is not reduced, and is not claimed.** Volume and timing correlate. A pinning service that
is the sole provider of a site sees every one of its readers, and no message in this protocol
changes that — the remedy is plurality of providers, which is a property of deployment rather than
of the wire.

**The host's side.** Sections 6.1 and 6.2 exist entirely for this: without them, any party could
enumerate what any machine hosts by asking it, at no cost and with no trace. That is a privacy
consideration for a person who is not a party to the connection at all — the publisher whose
readers are being counted — and it is the reason the omissions are normative rather than advisory.

**What survives them, which the impossibility analysis states in full.** An adversary can still
learn whether a peer holds one identifier it can already name, by asking for it and seeing whether
the block arrives. Serving is what the protocol is for and a success cannot be made ambiguous. The
narrowing is that each question now costs a transfer and can only be asked about content the
adversary can already name — not that the question has become unanswerable.

## Impossibility-and-capture analysis

**What this makes impossible.** Exactly one thing, and it is worth stating narrowly so that it
survives being checked: **serving a reader different content than the identifier names**. Not
"detectable" — impossible, because the reader computes the identifier itself and the bytes either
hash or they do not.

**What it does not make impossible, corrected.** An earlier version of this section also claimed
it is impossible to know what a peer holds without it choosing to tell you. That is false, and
**one request is enough to answer it**: name an identifier, see whether the block arrives. A peer
that holds it and serves it has told you, and serving is what the protocol is for. Sections 6.1
and 6.2 make a *refusal* indistinguishable; a *success* is perfectly distinguishable and always
will be.

So an adversary who knows a site's root identifier can determine whether a given peer hosts that
site, at the cost of one transfer, and nothing here prevents it. What 6.1 and 6.2 actually close
is the **cheap** oracle: bulk enumeration, and probing for existence without paying for the
transfer. An adversary must now spend bandwidth per question and can only ask about content it can
already name — which is a real and worthwhile narrowing, and a much smaller claim than the one it
replaced.

The correction is recorded rather than quietly edited because the claim was the kind this project
treats as a defect in its own right: a sentence that made VayuWeb sound more private than the
design supports. It read as thorough, it was checkable in one step, and nobody had checked it.

**What new thing becomes worth capturing.** Two things, and neither is small.

The first is **the discovery network**. Peers must find each other, and whoever operates the
mechanism by which they do sees who is looking for what. This is why 2.2 makes the Hyperswarm
binding explicitly non-normative — but non-normative is a licence to use something else, not a
guarantee that anybody will. The realistic outcome is that one discovery network becomes the one
everybody uses, and the specification's escape hatch goes unexercised. **This is the honest
weakness of the proposal** and it is not fixed by any text in it.

The second is **hosting concentration**. A site is fetchable from the peers that hold it, and the
easy way to make a site reliably fetchable is to pay one service to hold it. Nothing here prevents
that and nothing here should pretend to; what the protocol can do, and does, is refuse to make the
concentration invisible — 6.4 declines to print a reassuring availability figure over a site with
exactly one provider.

## Centralisation analysis

Parties whose withdrawal would degrade the mechanism:

1. **The discovery network operators**, under the reference binding. Withdrawal degrades peer
   discovery to whatever out-of-band means remain — a manually configured peer, a local network,
   an address written down. The protocol continues to work between peers that have found each
   other by any means, which is the property that keeps this from being a dependency rather than a
   convenience. Justified only in that sense, and it is a weaker justification than it first
   reads: see the capture analysis.

2. **Whoever holds the blocks.** A site with one provider has one party whose withdrawal removes
   it. This is not a defect of the protocol — content that nobody holds cannot be fetched by any
   design — but it is a genuine dependency and it is the reason 6.4 refuses to report an
   availability figure that would obscure it.

3. **The sha2-256 function**, shared with HOSTING.md and with the wider content-addressing
   ecosystem. Not a party, but a single point of failure of the same shape, and
   [CRYPTO-AGILITY.md](CRYPTO-AGILITY.md) is where its replacement is governed rather than here.

An analysis that found no dependent party would be an unfinished analysis, per VWIP-0000 3.2.
This one finds three, and the first is the one that should worry a reviewer.

## Migration and rollback

**There is nothing to migrate from.** No block-exchange path exists in any deployment, so this
adds a capability rather than changing one. A resolver that does not implement it continues to
serve locally imported content exactly as it does today, and MUST continue to be able to: the
capability is opt-in at the resolver, and a resolver with it disabled MUST NOT open a connection.

**Rollback is disabling it.** Because no state persists between connections and no format on disk
changes, a deployment that turns the protocol off returns to exactly its prior behaviour. Blocks
already fetched remain valid — they are content-addressed and their validity does not depend on
how they arrived.

**Forward compatibility.** `BHELLO.v` gates the session. A future version that changes message
semantics increments `v`; a future version that only adds message types does not, because 3.2
already requires an unknown `t` to be ignored rather than fatal.

## Activation epoch

**2027-08-06T00:00:00Z**, one year after creation and strictly in the future per Article 47.

The interval is not a formality. Category `Hosting` carries a 90-day minimum Review under
VWIP-0000 4.1, and a 30-day minimum Draft before it — but the binding constraint is Article 44.2
and VWIP-0000's own lifecycle: this proposal cannot reach Final without a second independent
implementation, and a wire protocol that has never been spoken between two independently written
implementations is a protocol whose ambiguities have not been found yet. The epoch is set far
enough out that the interoperability work is possible rather than nominal.

Nothing in this proposal may be relied upon before that epoch, and an implementation that ships
earlier MUST treat the wire format as unstable.

## Expiry of transitional mechanisms

**There are none, and the absence is deliberate.** No compatibility shim, no dual-stack period, no
"accept the old form until" clause — because there is no old form. Per Article 48 there is
accordingly no date to publish.

One thing that might be mistaken for a transitional mechanism is not one: fetching VayuWeb content
over bitswap from an ordinary IPFS node, permitted by the Motivation. That is a permanent
interoperability path, not a bridge with an end date, and it is permitted precisely because the
identifiers were chosen to make it possible. It is not, and never becomes, a way of satisfying
this specification.

## Test vectors

Observable on the wire, therefore mandatory. The vectors are **generated**, not transcribed:
`registry/scripts/generate-vectors.ts` emits them into `conformance/vectors.json` under the
`blockExchange` suite, and a test compares the committed artifact against a fresh generation, so
a change to any encoding rule shows up as a reviewable diff rather than as a silently different
expectation.

That distinction is the reason this section is short. An earlier draft of this proposal carried a
hand-typed hex block as an illustration of the shape. Per Article 44.6 a specification whose test
vectors are hand-written is a specification whose vectors have not been checked, and this corpus
has found enough of its own hand-written expectations to be wrong that the illustration was worth
less than nothing — it looked like evidence.

| Vector | What it pins | What a failure means |
|---|---|---|
| `blockx/bhello-v1` | `BHELLO` with `v=1`, `max=1048576` | The session opener does not round-trip. |
| `blockx/bhello-absurd-max` | `BHELLO` with `max=2^53 − 1` | The message decodes; a receiver that *allocates* from it has broken 5.1. |
| `blockx/bwant-one` | `BWANT` with one 36-byte identifier | The commonest message on the wire is wrong. |
| `blockx/bwant-at-the-limit` | `BWANT` with exactly 64 | The boundary is off by one in the restrictive direction. |
| `blockx/bwant-over-the-limit` | `BWANT` with 65 | An over-limit array was iterated rather than refused at decode. |
| `blockx/blocks-empty-leaf` | `BLOCKS` carrying the zero-byte raw leaf | A file of zero bytes is addressable, and a wire format that cannot carry it makes a published placeholder unfetchable. |
| `blockx/blocks-over-the-block-limit` | A block one octet over 1 MiB | The block bound is absent or applied after the identifier is computed. |
| `blockx/unknown-type` | A message with `t = "BHAVE"` | An unknown type closed the connection instead of dropping the message. |
| `blockx/bdone-held`, `blockx/bdone-absent` | `BDONE` from a peer holding the block and from one that is not | The two are distinguishable, and 6.2 is defeated by a side channel. |

Two notes on the set, both about how a vector can be worth less than it looks.

**The `BDONE` pair is the one to write first.** It is the only vector whose assertion is an
*equality* rather than a rejection, and equality assertions are the ones that pass for the wrong
reason. Both encodings are published rather than one plus a claim that the other matches, because
a claim is what a reader would have to take on trust.

**One vector is published as a recipe rather than as bytes.**
`blockx/blocks-over-the-block-limit` carries a `construct` field —
`{kind: "blocks-of-zeros", count: 1, bytes: 1048577}` — instead of a `message`. Written out, it
is 2.1 MB of hex zeros, of which every byte after the first carries no information: the
information in that vector is its *length*. A runner building the buffer from a stated length
tests exactly what a runner reading two million zeros would, and the artifact stays something a
reviewer can read. A vector nobody can read is a vector nobody checks.

## Status

Status: Draft. This document is the settling of the specification that
[docs/ROADMAP.md](../ROADMAP.md) Phase 4 requires before the code is written, on the roadmap's own
stated grounds that code written before the specification settles is code that will be thrown
away.

**The wire format is implemented; the protocol is not.** `registry/src/blockx.ts` holds the four
message encodings and the bounds of section 5, because the test vectors above are mandatory and a
vector nobody generated is a hex string somebody typed. Nothing else exists: no session, no
connection state, no outstanding-request accounting, no transport binding, and no fetch that
reaches another machine. The line is between what two implementations must agree on byte for byte
— fixed here — and behaviour this document constrains rather than defines, which waits for Draft
to end.
